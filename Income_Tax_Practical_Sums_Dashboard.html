Old Regime vs New Regime — what changes

Which regime do THESE practical sums use?

Old Regime. HRA exemption, most allowance exemptions (u/s 10(14)), and Chapter VI-A deductions (80C/80D/80TTA etc.) are only meaningful under the old regime — the new regime strips almost all of them out. Since your syllabus tests HRA/allowances/deductions computations, every worked sum and practice sum in this dashboard follows the old regime unless the question says otherwise.

Standard Deduction (salaried employees & pensioners)
RegimeAmount
Old Regime₹50,000
New Regime₹75,000
What the New Regime does NOT allow (main ones to remember)
  • No HRA exemption (Sec 10(13A))
  • No LTA exemption (Sec 10(5))
  • No Chapter VI-A deductions — 80C, 80D, 80TTA/80TTB, 80E, 80G etc. (except employer's NPS contribution u/s 80CCD(2) and a couple of others)
  • No deduction for interest on home loan — self-occupied property
  • Most Sec 10(14) special allowances lose exemption — a few survive: transport allowance for a disabled employee, conveyance allowance for official duty, and daily/tour allowance for official travel
What still works the SAME in both regimes
  • Gratuity exemption — Sec 10(10)
  • Leave encashment at retirement — Sec 10(10AA)
  • Commuted pension exemption — Sec 10(10A)
  • Employer's RPF contribution exemption (up to 12% of salary)
  • Standard deduction (amount differs, but both regimes allow it — see table above)
⚠ Exam trap: if a question doesn't mention "new regime" anywhere, assume Old Regime — that's the only regime where an HRA/allowances/deductions numerical makes sense to ask in the first place.

Charge of Income Tax — Sec. 4

Income tax is charged for every AY on the Total Income of the PREVIOUS year, at the rates prescribed by the annual Finance Act — this is why you always compute income for the PY but express the answer as "for A.Y. ____".

Capital Receipt vs Revenue Receipt — the four tests
TestRule
Net receipt vis-à-vis Gross receiptIncome means NET receipts, not gross — expenditure incurred to earn it is deductible per the Act
Due basis vis-à-vis Receipt basisIncome is taxable on accrual (due) basis for PGBP/Other Sources under mercantile system, or on receipt basis under cash system — some receipts (e.g. interest on enhanced compensation) are taxable only on receipt basis
Application vis-à-vis Diversion of Income"Application" = income first reaches the assessee, then he applies/spends it → fully taxable in his hands. "Diversion" = income is diverted by an overriding title BEFORE it reaches the assessee → not taxable in his hands
Capital vs Revenue natureCapital receipts (sale of a fixed asset, capital investment) are NOT taxable as income unless specifically brought under the Act (e.g. capital gains); revenue receipts (sales, salary, rent, interest) ARE taxable
Agricultural Income — Sec. 10(1) & partial integration (Rule 7)

Agricultural income itself is fully exempt from tax. But if BOTH conditions below are met, it is still added back temporarily just to push the assessee into a higher tax bracket ("partial integration") — this only applies to individuals/HUF/AOP/BOI under the old regime:

  • Net agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000, AND
  • Non-agricultural Total Income exceeds the basic exemption limit
Step 1: Tax on (Non-agri income + Agri income) at slab rates Step 2: Tax on (Basic exemption limit + Agri income) at slab rates Step 3: Tax payable = Step 1 − Step 2
Casual Income — Sec. 2(24)(ix)

Winnings from lotteries, crossword puzzles, races (including horse races), card games, gambling/betting, game shows. Taxed flatly at 30% (+cess) under "Income from Other Sources" — no basic exemption, no Chapter VI-A deduction, and no expense is allowed against it.

Method of Accounting — Sec. 145

Cash system: income taxed when actually received. Mercantile (accrual) system: income taxed when it accrues/becomes due, whether received or not. Salary is always taxed on "due or received, whichever is earlier" basis, regardless of the employer's accounting method.

⚠ Exam trap: examiners love a one-line question like "Define Assessment Year" or "What is Gross Total Income vis-à-vis Total Income" tucked inside a bigger practical question — know these definitions cold even though the main marks are for the numerical.

1 · Residential Status (Sec. 6)

Chapter 1 · Unit I5 marks
Basic conditions — need at least ONE
  • (a) In India 182 days or more in the PY, OR
  • (b) In India 60 days or more in the PY and 365 days or more in the 4 years immediately before the PY
Exceptions — when the "60 days" in (b) is relaxed
Who60 days replaced by
Indian citizen leaving India for employment abroad, or as a crew member of an Indian ship182 days
Indian citizen / Person of Indian Origin (PIO) on a visit to India — Indian income ≤ ₹15 lakh182 days
Indian citizen / PIO on a visit to India — Indian income > ₹15 lakh120 days
Deemed Resident — Sec. 6(1A)

An Indian citizen whose India-sourced income exceeds ₹15 lakh AND who is not liable to tax in any other country by reason of domicile/residence is a "deemed resident" — even with 0 days in India. He is always classified RNOR.

Conclusion
  • No basic condition satisfied → Non-Resident (NR)
  • At least one basic condition satisfied → Resident; then test the additional conditions below
Additional conditions — need BOTH for ROR
  • Resident in India in 2 of the preceding 10 years, AND
  • In India for 730 days or more in the preceding 7 years

Both satisfied → ROR. Either one fails → RNOR.

⚠ Exam trap: a citizen/PIO who becomes "Resident" ONLY through the 120-day exception is automatically RNOR — the additional conditions are not even tested for him.
Worked Sum — basic exception (employment abroad)
Q. Mr. Arjun, an Indian citizen, left India for the first time on 15th September 2025 to take up employment in Dubai. Determine his residential status for A.Y. 2026-27.
Step 1 — Count days in India in PY 2025-26
1 Apr–15 Sept 2025: Apr(30)+May(31)+Jun(30)+Jul(31)+Aug(31)+Sep(15) = 168 days
Step 2 — Condition (a)
168 days < 182 days → NOT satisfied
Step 3 — Condition (b) with exception
He is an Indian citizen leaving India for employment abroad → the 60-day limb is replaced by 182 days → he is nowhere near 182 days present anyway, so this condition also fails
Step 4 — Conclusion
No basic condition satisfied → Non-Resident (NR) for A.Y. 2026-27
Answer: Mr. Arjun is a Non-Resident (NR) for A.Y. 2026-27.
Practice 1 — plain basic conditions standard case
Q. Ms. Kavya, a foreign national, came to India for the first time on 10th June 2021. During PY 2021-22 to 2025-26 she stayed in India for 100, 190, 40, 200 and 95 days respectively. Determine her residential status for A.Y. 2026-27.
Step 1 — Current PY 2025-26
95 days in India → condition (a): 95 < 182, not satisfied
Step 2 — Preceding 4 years (2021-22 to 2024-25)
100+190+40+200 = 530 days ≥ 365 days → condition (b) satisfied (she is not covered by any exception, so plain 60-day rule applies and 95≥60 too)
Step 3 — She is Resident. Check additional conditions
Resident in 2 of preceding 10 years? Yes (2022-23 & 2023-24 onward were resident years) → satisfied. Days in preceding 7 years (2018-19 to 2024-25): only 2021-22 to 2024-25 apply = 100+190+40+200 = 530 days < 730 days → NOT satisfied
Step 4 — Conclusion
One additional condition fails → Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)
Answer: RNOR for A.Y. 2026-27.
Practice 2 — PIO visit, income > ₹15 lakh 120-day exception
Q. Mr. Rahul, an Indian citizen settled in Singapore, comes on a visit to India during PY 2025-26 and stays for 130 days. His India-sourced income (excluding foreign income) is ₹18,00,000. He was in India for 400 days during the 4 years preceding PY 2025-26. Determine his residential status for A.Y. 2026-27.
Step 1 — Condition (a)
130 days < 182 days → NOT satisfied
Step 2 — Condition (b), which limb applies?
He is a citizen visiting India with Indian income > ₹15 lakh → 60-day limb replaced by 120 days
Step 3 — Apply the 120-day test
130 days ≥ 120 days ✓ AND 400 days ≥ 365 days (preceding 4 years) ✓ → condition (b) satisfied
Step 4 — Resident; but which category?
Since he became Resident ONLY via the 120-day exception, he is automatically classified RNOR — additional conditions are not tested (exam trap rule above)
Answer: Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR) for A.Y. 2026-27.
Practice 3 — Deemed Resident Sec. 6(1A)
Q. Mr. Anand, an Indian citizen, has India-sourced income of ₹20,00,000 for PY 2025-26. He is not liable to pay tax in any other country by reason of domicile or residence. He visited India only for 25 days during the year and was outside India for the whole of the preceding 4 years. Determine his residential status for A.Y. 2026-27.
Step 1 — Test ordinary basic conditions
25 days < 182 → (a) fails. 0 days in preceding 4 years → (b) fails.
Not resident under the normal Sec. 6(1) test.
Step 2 — Check Sec. 6(1A) deeming fiction
He is an Indian citizen, India income (₹20,00,000) > ₹15 lakh, and not liable to tax anywhere else → conditions for deemed residency are met
Step 3 — Conclusion
He is a "Deemed Resident" u/s 6(1A) — and by law is always classified RNOR
Answer: Deemed Resident, treated as RNOR for A.Y. 2026-27.

2 · Gratuity — Sec. 10(10)

Chapter 2 · Unit II5 marks
Three categories of employee
CategoryExemption
Govt. employee (Central/State/Local authority)Fully exempt — no ceiling
Non-govt., covered under Payment of Gratuity Act 1972Least of: (i) actual gratuity (ii) 15/26 × last drawn salary (Basic+DA) × completed years of service (round UP part-year > 6 months) (iii) ₹20,00,000
Non-govt., NOT covered under the ActLeast of: (i) actual gratuity (ii) ½ × average salary of last 10 months × completed years of service (fraction of a year ignored) (iii) ₹20,00,000
Exception — more than one employer / lifetime ceiling

The ₹20,00,000 ceiling is a lifetime aggregate across all employers, not per job. If exemption was already claimed against an earlier gratuity, the remaining ceiling for this employer = ₹20,00,000 − amount already exempted.

⚠ Exam trap: "Salary" for the 15/26 formula = Basic + DA (only if DA is under terms of employment) — never include bonus, commission (unless it's a fixed % of turnover for non-Act cases), or perquisites.
Worked Sum — covered under the Act
Q. Mr. Rakesh retires from a private company covered under the Payment of Gratuity Act after 24 years 7 months of service. Last drawn salary (Basic + DA) = ₹40,000 p.m. Gratuity received = ₹9,50,000. Compute taxable gratuity.
Step 1 — Completed years
24 years 7 months → part year > 6 months → rounded up to 25 years
Step 2 — Apply formula (ii)
15/26 × 40,000 × 25 = ₹5,76,923
Step 3 — Compare all three limits
LimitAmount (₹)
(i) Actual gratuity received9,50,000
(ii) 15/26 × salary × years5,76,923
(iii) Statutory ceiling20,00,000
Exempt = least of the three5,76,923
Step 4 — Taxable gratuity
9,50,000 − 5,76,923 = ₹3,73,077
Answer: Taxable gratuity = ₹3,73,077 (exempt ₹5,76,923)

3 · Pension & Commuted Pension — Sec. 10(10A)

Chapter 2 · Unit II5 marks
Uncommuted pension

Always fully taxable under "Income from Salary" for everyone (govt. or non-govt., commuted a part or not).

Commuted pension
Employee typeExemption
Government employeeFully exempt
Non-govt., also receives gratuity⅓ × full (100%) value of commuted pension
Non-govt., does NOT receive gratuity½ × full (100%) value of commuted pension

Full value = (commuted amount received ÷ % commuted) × 100.

Exception — Family pension different head entirely

Pension received by legal heirs after the employee's death is "Family Pension" — taxed under Income from Other Sources, NOT Salary (no employer-employee relationship with the recipient). Deduction u/s 57(iia) = least of ⅓ of family pension or a fixed ceiling.

RegimeCeiling
Old regime₹15,000
New regime (FY 2024-25 onward)₹25,000
⚠ Exam trap: commuted pension of a govt. employee is fully exempt regardless of gratuity; only for non-govt. employees does "did he also get gratuity?" decide ⅓ vs ½.
Worked Sum — non-govt., with gratuity
Q. Mr. Suresh, a non-government employee, also received gratuity on retirement. He was entitled to a monthly pension of ₹8,000. On 1st October he commuted 40% of his pension and received ₹2,40,000. Compute the amount of pension taxable for the previous year (Apr–Mar), assuming uncommuted pension continued for the rest of the year at the reduced rate.
Step 1 — Uncommuted pension, Apr–Sep (6 months, full rate)
6 × 8,000 = ₹48,000 (fully taxable)
Step 2 — Uncommuted pension, Oct–Mar (6 months, reduced 60%)
60% of 8,000 = 4,800; 6 × 4,800 = ₹28,800 (fully taxable)
Step 3 — Full value of commuted pension
2,40,000 ÷ 40 × 100 = ₹6,00,000
Step 4 — Exemption (gratuity also received → ⅓ rule)
⅓ × 6,00,000 = ₹2,00,000 exempt
Step 5 — Taxable commuted pension
2,40,000 − 2,00,000 = ₹40,000
Step 6 — Total taxable pension
48,000 + 28,800 + 40,000 = ₹1,16,800
Answer: Total taxable pension for the year = ₹1,16,800

Practice 3 — Family pension Income from Other Sources
Q. Mrs. Sunita receives family pension of ₹18,000 per month after the death of her husband (a non-government employee) during PY 2025-26. Compute the taxable amount and state under which head it is taxed (use the old-regime ceiling).

Step 2 — Annual receipt

4 · Calculation of Taxable / Total Income

Chapter 1 · Unit I5 marks
Formula

Gross Total Income (GTI) = Salary + House Property + Business/Profession + Capital Gains + Other Sources (after inter-head set-off of losses).
Total Income = GTI − Deductions under Chapter VI-A (Sec. 80C to 80U), rounded off to the nearest ₹10.

Common Chapter VI-A deductions to remember
SectionForMax limit
80CLIC, PF, ELSS, tuition fees, etc.₹1,50,000
80DMedical insurance (self & family, non-senior)₹25,000
80DMedical insurance (senior citizen)₹50,000
80TTASavings account interest (non-senior)₹10,000
80TTBInterest income (senior citizen — any deposit)₹50,000
⚠ Exam trap: total Chapter VI-A deduction can never exceed the GTI — it cannot create or increase a loss. If GTI is less than eligible deduction, Total Income is restricted to ₹0, not a negative figure.
Worked Sum
Q. From the following particulars of Mr. Nikhil for A.Y. 2026-27, compute his Total Income: Income from Salary ₹6,40,000; Income from House Property ₹1,20,000; Income from Other Sources ₹35,000; Deduction u/s 80C (LIC + PF) ₹1,10,000 (max allowed ₹1,50,000); Deduction u/s 80D (medical insurance) ₹18,000.

Head of Income₹
Income from Salary6,40,000
Income from House Property1,20,000
Income from Other Sources35,000
Gross Total Income7,95,000
Step 2 — Deduct Chapter VI-A deductions
80C: ₹1,10,000 (within ₹1,50,000 cap, allowed in full) 80D: ₹18,000 Total deductions = 1,10,000 + 18,000 = ₹1,28,000

Q. Mr. Deepak has: Income from Salary ₹4,80,000; Income from House Property (loss) −₹40,000; Income from Other Sources ₹60,000; Deduction u/s 80C ₹1,50,000. Compute Total Income.

Practice 2 — 80TTA + 80D together multiple deductions
Q. Mr. Verma has: Income from Salary ₹5,25,000; Interest from Savings Bank A/c ₹12,000; Income from House Property ₹80,000; Deduction u/s 80C ₹95,000; u/s 80D (self, non-senior) ₹22,000 (max ₹25,000); u/s 80TTA (savings interest, max ₹10,000). Compute Total Income.

80C = 95,000 (within cap) 80D = min(22,000, 25,000) = 22,000 80TTA = min(12,000, 10,000) = 10,000 Total = 95,000 + 22,000 + 10,000 = ₹1,27,000
Step 3 — Total Income
6,17,000 − 1,27,000 = ₹4,90,000

Practice 3 — deduction restricted to GTI floor at ₹0
Q. Mr. Iqbal (a part-time worker) has Gross Total Income of only ₹1,20,000, and he actually invested ₹1,50,000 eligible for deduction u/s 80C. Compute his Total Income.
Step 1 — Deduction cannot exceed GTI
Eligible investment ₹1,50,000, but GTI itself is only ₹1,20,000 → deduction restricted to ₹1,20,000
Step 2 — Total Income
1,20,000 − 1,20,000 = ₹0

Income from Salary — Full Computation

Chapter 2 · Unit II15 marks
Statement format

Basic Pay + DA + Bonus/Commission + taxable part of Allowances + taxable part of Perquisites + taxable part of Gratuity/Pension + employer's excess PF contribution = Gross Salary; less Standard Deduction = Income from Salary. Standard deduction is ₹50,000 under the Old Regime (₹75,000 under the New Regime, but the New Regime doesn't allow HRA/allowance exemptions, so these worked sums use ₹50,000).

Perquisite — Rent-Free Accommodation (Rule 3)
CaseValuation
Government employeeLicense fee fixed by the government
Non-govt., employer-owned, city population > 40 lakh10% of salary
Non-govt., employer-owned, population 15–40 lakh7.5% of salary
Non-govt., employer-owned, population < 15 lakh5% of salary
Employer-leased (not owned)Lower of actual lease rent paid, or 10% of salary

All figures above are net of any rent recovered from the employee. If furnished, add 10% p.a. of the cost of furniture (or actual hire charges) to the value.

Perquisite — Motor Car (Rule 3(2), both official + personal use, expenses met by employer)
Engine capacityValue/month+ Driver
Up to 1.6 litre₹1,800+ ₹900/month

If used exclusively for official purposes (properly documented) → NIL perquisite. If exclusively personal → actual running/maintenance cost + 10% p.a. depreciation, less amount recovered from employee.

Other frequently-tested exceptions
  • Leave encashment during service (while still employed) → fully taxable, no exemption at all.
  • Leave encashment at retirement (non-govt.) → exempt up to the least of: actual amount, 10 months' average salary, cash equivalent of leave balance (max 30 days per year of service), or ₹25,00,000.
  • LTA (Leave Travel Allowance) → exempt to the extent of actual eligible travel cost by shortest route (economy air / AC-I rail), subject to conditions — balance taxable.
  • Employer's contribution to Recognised PF → exempt up to 12% of salary (Basic+DA); excess is taxable.
⚠ Exam trap: "Salary" for RFA/PF/gratuity purposes usually means Basic + DA (terms) + fixed commission — it excludes the value of perquisites themselves and most allowances, unless the question states otherwise.
